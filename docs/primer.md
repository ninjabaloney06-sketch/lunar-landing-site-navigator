# Lunar south pole primer

Phase 1 learning material (general preparation only, no project design). Every number below links to its source; the sources were checked to load on 24 Oct 2026. Lines marked **(derived)** are arithmetic on cited numbers and need S's sign-off before we rely on them.

## Why lighting is extreme at the poles

- The Moon's equator is nearly aligned with the ecliptic. Its rotation axis is tilted **6.68°** to its orbit, and the orbit is inclined **5.145°** to the ecliptic ([NASA NSSDC Moon Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html)), so the equator is tilted only ≈ **1.54°** to the ecliptic plane **(derived: 6.68° − 5.145°)**.
- The subsolar point (solar zenith point) therefore never leaves a narrow band around the equator. In NASA SVS's hourly 2026 "Dial-A-Moon" data the subsolar latitude ranges from **−1.557° to +1.586°** ([mooninfo_2026.json](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/vis/a000000/a005500/a005588/mooninfo_2026.json), behind [SVS 5588](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5588)).
- At the pole, solar elevation above a flat, airless horizon equals the subsolar latitude **(derived)**: the Sun circles the horizon within about ±1.6° and never climbs higher. Terrain relief of a few tens of meters then decides lit versus shadowed — "Peaks of near-eternal sunlight tower above nearby permanently shadowed regions (PSRs)" ([Barker et al. 2023, PSJ 4, 183, open access CC BY 4.0](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1)). Poleward of 80° S the total PSR area is **20,800 km²** (same paper).
- Consequence for the challenge: lighting at a south-pole site is a *terrain* question, so it must be answered with a horizon mask over a DEM (below), cross-checked against published illumination products ([Mazarico et al.; see data guide](data-guide.md)).

## Why Earth hovers near the horizon

- The sub-Earth point is "the lunar latitude at which the Earth is directly overhead" ([NASA SVS](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5199)). Libration in latitude — an apparent north–south nodding of the Moon — is driven mainly by the **6.68°** angle between the Moon's equator and orbital planes (same SVS page). Over time "up to 59%" of the surface becomes observable from Earth ([NASA Stargaze: Librations of the Moon](https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/stargaze/Smoon4.htm)).
- In the 2026 SVS data the sub-Earth latitude ranges from **−6.795° to +6.856°** (about ±7°); sub-Earth longitude ranges **−7.936° to +7.594°** (same JSON as above).
- At the south pole, Earth's elevation above a flat horizon equals the sub-Earth latitude **(derived)**: Earth stays within about ±7° of the horizon and is *below* it during the part of the ~monthly libration cycle when the sub-Earth point lies in the Moon's northern hemisphere. A polar direct-to-Earth link therefore needs a horizon-clear direction toward Earth during the specific window, not just "Earth is up".

## What direct-to-Earth (DTE) comms needs

- An unobstructed line of sight from the site to Earth for the whole communications window. Near the pole Earth is always low (previous section), so terrain in Earth's azimuth can break the link even when Earth is geometrically above the horizon.
- NASA treats this as a first-class landing criterion: "communication capabilities with Earth" is one of the factors named in selecting the Artemis south-pole landing regions ([NASA news release, 28 Oct 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-artemis-iii-moon-landing-regions)).
- Published reference products exist for exactly this question: PGDA's lunar polar illumination/visibility maps include an "Average Earth visibility" product for "mission planning"/"radio contact", computed from LOLA topography, in which "the Earth is considered visible if any part of its disc is not obscured" ([PGDA product 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69)).
- Relays (orbiters, surface nodes, e.g. the LTE demo flown on IM-2) are the standard workaround when DTE fails; for our tool the question stays "when is DTE possible".

## What a horizon mask is

- A **horizon mask** for a site is the terrain-caused elevation limit as a function of azimuth: for each of 360 compass directions around the site, the lowest elevation angle at which local terrain blocks the sky. It is built by casting rays from the site over a DEM (e.g. the LOLA south-pole LDEM) out to tens of kilometers and taking the maximum terrain elevation angle along each ray, lunar curvature included **(method description; the same construction underlies the published LOLA-derived products cited here)**.
- Visibility rule: an object is terrain-visible at time t iff elevation(t) > mask(azimuth(t)) — for the Sun often refined to "any part of the disk", for Earth likewise (PGDA's Earth-visibility definition above). This is how published illumination and Earth-visibility maps are made from LOLA topography ([Mazarico et al. 2011 via PGDA](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69); [Barker et al. 2023](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1) for the reference DEM).

## Coordinate conventions (draft for S to check)

SPICE carries two lunar body-fixed frames, defined in NAIF's Moon frame kernel [`moon_de440_250416.tf`](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/generic_kernels/fk/satellites/moon_de440_250416.tf) (see also the [NAIF Frames Required Reading](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/toolkit_docs/C/req/frames.html)):

- **MOON_PA** (principal axes, frame ID 31000): axes are the Moon's principal axes. Per the kernel, "the Z axis of this system does not coincide with the Moon's mean spin axis, nor does the X axis coincide with the mean direction to the center of the Earth". This is the frame the lunar orientation PCK (`moon_pa_de440_200625.bpc`) natively delivers.
- **MOON_ME** (mean Earth/polar axis, frame ID 31001): "+Z axis ... aligned with the north mean lunar rotation axis, while the prime meridian contains the mean Earth direction" — "also sometimes called the 'mean Earth/mean rotation axis' system" (same kernel). This is the system the IAU/IAG cartographic working group uses for lunar coordinates (the kernel cites the IAU "Report on Cartographic Coordinates and Rotational Elements: 2009").
- The two differ by a fixed rotation: for DE440 the angle is "approximately **0.02886 degrees** ... approximately **875 m** ... as a displacement along a great circle on the Moon's surface" (same kernel). Mixing them silently shifts every site by ~0.9 km and rotates the horizon mask — the classic error.

**Which frame a tool like ours uses (draft, S to confirm):** do all cartography — site latitude/longitude, DEM sampling, horizon mask, map display — in **MOON_ME**, because the data we would consume is published in it: "All the LOLA polar products use a stereographic projection centered at the pole in the MOON_ME frame of the JPL ephemeris DE421" ([Barker et al. 2023](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1); likewise the [PGDA product 90 page](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90): files "in the MOON_ME reference frame of the JPL DE421 ephemeris"), and IAU lunar coordinates are mean-Earth by definition. Compute Sun/Earth positions with the DE440 SPK + `moon_pa_de440_200625.bpc` and let the frame kernel transform vectors into MOON_ME before any local-horizon math. Never feed a longitude/latitude from one system into the other, and note the version nuance: our kernels are DE440-based while the LOLA products are ME-of-DE421; the frame kernel ships `MOON_ME_DE440_ME421`, "closely aligned with the mean Earth/polar axis frame of DE421", precisely to bridge that (exact residual offset: S to read from the kernel's comparison section).
