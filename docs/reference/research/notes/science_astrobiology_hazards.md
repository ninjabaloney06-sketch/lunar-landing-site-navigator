## 1. Astrobiology and microbes: is there life on the Moon, and can terrestrial microbes survive there?

### Takeaway
There is **no evidence of indigenous lunar life** (Apollo quarantine/biohazard testing found none; NASA's 2026 study authors state the Moon has no known liquid water or other ingredients for growth). What exists is (a) a history of **terrestrial contamination claims** (Surveyor 3 — now regarded as lab contamination), and (b) 2025-2026 **modelling** showing that shadowed south-polar terrain is one of the least biocidal environments on the Moon, so that *terrestrial* spores/cells brought by landers and crews could stay viable (dormant, not growing) for days to decades. The "possible bacteria" topic is therefore a forward-contamination / sample-integrity issue, not a detection of lunar organisms.

**Apollo quarantine (indigenous life)**
- Quarantine/biohazard clearance testing of Apollo 11, 12 and 14 samples "revealed no evidence of life or hazard" and quarantine was discontinued after Apollo 14; NASA and the Interagency Committee on Back-Contamination concluded there was no hazard. The test battery was based on the Baylor protocol, which was never fully implemented. [ESTABLISHED] — [NASA NTRS 20120000717](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20120000717/downloads/20120000717.pdf); [NASA NTRS 20140005965](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140005965/downloads/20140005965.pdf)
- For Apollo 15-17 the Lunar Receiving Laboratory operated as a curation rather than quarantine facility. [ESTABLISHED] — [NASA SP-4214 ch. 12-9](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/SP-4214/ch12-9.html)
- Amino acids found in Apollo regolith (early studies: up to ~70 ppb) are "primarily terrestrial" contamination with a minor meteoritic contribution; lunar-module exhaust was not a primary source (Elsila et al. 2016, GCA). [ESTABLISHED for those samples] — [Elsila et al. 2016 (NASA GSFC copy)](https://ael.gsfc.nasa.gov/sed/content/uploadFiles/publication_files/Elsila2016.pdf); [ScienceDaily 2015](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/10/151029102253.htm)
- Apollo spacecraft hardware surfaces were cleaned to organic contamination levels of 10-140 ng/cm2 (context for how much terrestrial organic material reached the Moon). — [Glavin et al. 2010 (NASA GSFC copy)](https://cisto-news.gsfc.nasa.gov/sed/content/uploadFiles/publication_files/Glavin%20et%20al.%20Moon%2020101.pdf)

**Surveyor 3 / *Streptococcus mitis***
- Claim: *S. mitis* cultured from the Surveyor 3 camera returned by Apollo 12 was interpreted as having survived ~2.5-3 years on the Moon. Rebuttal: Rummel, Allton & Morrison (2011), "A Microbe on the Moon? Surveyor III and Lessons Learned for Future Sample Return Missions", concluded the team "only detected their own contamination"; handling problems included short-sleeve scrubs (exposed arms) and return of the camera in a nylon duffel bag rather than a sealed container. NASA officials no longer support the survival claim; the test cannot be repeated because the parts were re-exposed to terrestrial conditions. [CONTESTED -> regarded as refuted] — [Space.com](https://www.space.com/11536-moon-microbe-mystery-solved-apollo-12.html); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna42929708); [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/13999-moon-microbe-mystery-solved-apollo-12.html); [Wikipedia: Reports of Streptococcus mitis on the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reports_of_Streptococcus_mitis_on_the_Moon)

**Apollo human-waste bags**
- 96 bags of urine, faeces and other waste were left at the six Apollo sites (all near-equatorial, none polar). A. Schuerger (Univ. Florida) called microbial survival "highly improbable" though the best odds of anything landed; M. Lupisella (NASA Goddard) proposed a robotic sample retrieval. No funded retrieval mission was found. [Survival: SPECULATIVE, untested] — [BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/space/why-is-there-poo-on-the-moon/); [SYFY Wire](https://www.syfy.com/syfywire/nasa-wants-poop-apollo-left-on-moon)

**Beresheet tardigrades (2019)**
- Beresheet crashed on 11 Apr 2019 carrying dehydrated tardigrades (Arch Mission "Lunar Library"). Traspas & Burchell (2021, *Astrobiology*) fired tardigrades in nylon sabots: survival up to ~0.9 km/s (reported 825-900 m/s), death above ~1.14 GPa shock; the lander's metal frame hitting the surface would have produced shock "well above" 1.14 GPa, so survival is unlikely. [Lab-ESTABLISHED limit; survival on Moon unlikely] — [CosmoQuest](https://cosmoquest.org/x/2021/05/tardigrades-designed-for-high-impact-collisions); [EarthSky](https://earthsky.org/human-world/tardigrades-lunar-library-beresheet-spacecraft-crash-moon); [Wikipedia: Tardigrades on the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tardigrades_on_the_Moon)

**Chang'e-4 biosphere experiment (2019)**
- Sealed, unheated "lunar micro ecosystem" (Chongqing Univ., Xie Gengxin): cotton seeds sprouted — the only organism confirmed to respond; later 3-D reconstruction indicates two leaves. The sprout died at the first lunar night (~14 Earth days) when the canister cooled to about -52 C and lower (reports of -190 C outside). Peer-reviewed write-up: *Acta Astronautica* 2023, "The first biological experiment on lunar surface for Humankind: Device and results". Relevance: growth occurred only inside a pressurised, lit, watered container — not in the lunar environment. [ESTABLISHED] — [Universe Today](https://universetoday.com/143614/chinas-lander-successfully-grew-some-cotton-plants-on-the-moon-fruit-flies-and-potatoes-didnt-fare-so-well/); [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/china-grew-cotton-plants-on-the-moon-they-died-in-the-darkness-and-the-cold-778); [Phys.org 2023](https://phys.org/news/2023-10-china-tiny-farm-moon.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a site-scoring app, "possible bacteria" should be encoded as a **contamination-sensitivity / contamination-persistence** attribute (higher inside or adjacent to PSRs and persistent shadow), not as a "life potential" score. Sites with large shadowed fractions near the lander (Shackleton rim/Connecting Ridge, de Gerlache Rim, Nobile Rim, Faustini rim) both attract volatile science and maximise persistence of crew-borne microbes.
- All positive survival statements are model outputs built on laboratory tolerance limits; there is no in-situ lunar measurement of microbial viability. The only "field" data points (Surveyor 3, waste bags, Beresheet) are respectively refuted, untested and judged unlikely.

## 2. Prebiotic / organic interest: volatiles and organics in PSRs, cold-trap record, "Earth's attic"

### Takeaway
The single ground-truth data point is LCROSS (Cabeus, 2009): water at 5.6 +/- 2.9 wt% plus a mix of other volatiles including light hydrocarbons, sulphur species, ammonia and CO2, consistent with a cold-trapped cometary/asteroidal contribution. PSRs are valued as a possible multi-billion-year record of volatile and organic delivery to the inner solar system — but no in-situ PSR chemistry exists, and crater-age work suggests the largest PSR floors may not retain the *oldest* ice. "Earth's attic" (ancient terrestrial meteorites preserved on the Moon) is a modelled expectation (~7 ppm) with one contested sample candidate.

## 3. Planetary protection and site-protection rules (COSPAR, NASA, Artemis Accords, proposed protected sites)

### Takeaway
No rule forbids landing in or near south-polar PSRs. The whole Moon is COSPAR Category II (documentation only); since 2021 surface missions are split into **IIa** (most of the Moon; report propulsion-released volatiles) and **IIb** (missions accessing PSRs and the lunar poles, defined as **south of 79 S** and north of 86 N; full organic inventory, solids and volatiles). NASA implements this through NPR 8715.24 and NASA-STD-8719.27; lunar missions are exempt from bioburden control. Every candidate site poleward of 80 S is therefore Category IIb. Protected "scientific sites" on the Moon are proposals only (IAU working group; SESI concept); the Artemis Accords add temporary "safety zones" and heritage preservation.

**Lander exhaust contaminating polar ice**
- Prem et al. 2020 (JGR Planets, DOI 10.1029/2020JE006464), modelling a ~1,200 kg lander: water-vapour exhaust is globally redistributed within hours; 30-40% remained in the exosphere/surface after two months; roughly 20% ultimately freezes out near the poles; cold-trap contamination scales with exhaust mass and proximity of the landing site to the poles; water is only ~1/3 of typical exhaust and the "stickiness" of water on regolith is uncertain. [MODEL] — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/will-increasing-traffic-to-the-moon-contaminate-its-precious-ice/); [Prem et al. 2020](https://zendy.io/title/10.1029/2020je006464)
- NASEM 2020: Finding 6 — no clear prioritised science objectives for PSRs exist, which is needed for an effective policy; organic volatiles from landings and habitat venting are the most likely confounders; most combustion products are not organic molecules and modelled deposited concentrations may be very low; most contaminants are not expected to penetrate more than a few centimetres, so sampling at depth is less affected; suggested mitigations: mission-specific exhaust modelling, characterised exhaust signatures, witness plates, sampling protocols. — [NASEM 2020, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26029/chapter/6)
- NASA follow-on modelling topics (2024): organic footprint of lunar landing vehicles, descent-engine plume-induced contamination, free-molecular Monte Carlo transport/deposition of contaminants in PSRs. — [NASA OPP slides to NASEM](https://www.nationalacademies.org/cdn/materials/a172f18a-07ad-4ab0-a14e-bdc86f0b2d32)

**Artemis Accords and proposed protected sites**
- Artemis Accords Section 11: "safety zones" whose size, scope and duration reflect the nature of operations and which "will be temporary"; signatories commit to respect free access (Outer Space Treaty) and due regard. Section 9: preserve outer-space heritage — "historically significant human or robotic landing sites, artifacts, spacecraft, and other evidence of activity". Legal scholars note tension with the non-appropriation principle. — [NASA Artemis Accords](https://www.nasa.gov/artemis-accords/); [ASIL Insights 24(31)](https://www.asil.org/insights/volume/24/issue/31/artemis-accords-and-future-international-space-law)
- IAU Working Group on astronomy from the Moon: authorised 15 Sep 2023 under Commission C.B7; aims to protect radio observations in the Shielded Zone of the Moon (SZM) via ITU-R and to identify, prioritise and develop policy to protect sites of extreme scientific value. — [IAU WG triennial report 2021-2024](https://www.iau.org/static/science/scientific_bodies/working_groups/342/wg-342-triennial-report-2021-2024.pdf)
- "Sites of Extraordinary Scientific Importance" (SESIs): two main classes discussed — the far-side radio-quiet zone and polar cold traps; advocates (e.g., M. Elvis, J. Burns, I. Crawford) argue specific polar craters and far-side locations should receive a formal designation. Status: proposal only, no binding regime. [PROPOSAL] — [Space.com](https://www.space.com/moon-perfect-for-lunar-astronomy-protection); [IAU paper to COPUOS STSC 2024 (A/AC.105/C.1/2024/CRP.14)](https://www.unoosa.org/res/oosadoc/data/documents/2024/aac_105c_12024crp/aac_105c_12024crp_14_0_html/AC105_C1_2024_CRP14E.pdf)
- ITU-R Recommendation RA.479: the far side's shielded zone is the only part of the lunar surface always protected from Earth-generated radio interference. — [ITU-R RA.479-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/ra/R-REC-RA.479-5-200305-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- A NASA Lunar Surface Science Workshop session (2020) on "Planetary Protection & PSR Classification" discussed whether all PSRs are equal and suggested assessing protection as a function of science (e.g., pairing similar PSRs, one for science and one for resource use) rather than fixed keep-out zones. — [LSSW report](https://lunarscience.arc.nasa.gov/lssw/downloads/LSSW-Planetary-Protection-_-PSR-Classification-Report_Final.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rule for the app: **every site with latitude <= -79 is COSPAR/NASA Category IIb** (Shackleton, de Gerlache, Haworth, Shoemaker, Faustini, Nobile, Cabeus, Malapert, Mons Mouton all qualify). The consequence is paperwork (volatile + full organic inventory, pre-launch and end-of-mission), not a landing prohibition and not sterilisation.
- A scientifically motivated (non-regulatory) penalty can be justified for landing *inside or immediately upwind of* a high-value PSR, scaled by exhaust mass (a Starship-class HLS emits orders of magnitude more exhaust than the ~1,200 kg lander modelled by Prem et al.) — this is an inference from Prem et al. and NASEM, not a published rule.
- No heritage sites (Artemis Accords Sec. 9 / NID II-L "historic sites") lie in the south polar region other than recent impact/landing hardware (LCROSS at Cabeus, Chandrayaan-1 MIP near Shackleton, IM-2 on Mons Mouton); whether these count as protected heritage is undefined in the sources found.

## 4. Science priorities by location (Artemis III SDT objectives, SPA basin, crater ages, geology of candidate regions, pits, geophysics/astronomy)

### Takeaway
The Artemis III SDT (2020) set seven objectives; site value is tied to proximity to PSRs and to South Pole-Aitken (SPA) basin materials. SPA is now sample-dated at ~4.25 Ga (Chang'e-6); Mons Mouton and Malapert are interpreted (not proven) as SPA rim remnants; the big PSR craters Haworth, Shoemaker and Faustini are pre-Nectarian (~4.1-4.2 Ga) while Shackleton is Late Imbrian (~3.5 Ga). Published site-specific geology exists mainly for the Shackleton rim / Connecting Ridge area. No lunar pit or lava-tube skylight is catalogued poleward of 80 S.

### Cited Findings

**Artemis III Science Definition Team report (Weber, Cohen, Lawrence, chairs; submitted 6 Nov 2020, public 4 Dec 2020)**
- Seven objectives: (1) Understanding planetary processes; (2) Understanding the character and origin of lunar polar volatiles; (3) Interpreting the impact history of the Earth-Moon system; (4) Revealing the record of the ancient Sun and our astronomical environment; (5) Observing the universe and the local space environment from a unique location; (6) Conducting experimental science in the lunar environment; (7) Investigating and mitigating exploration risks. — [Universe Today summary](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/here-are-nasas-science-priorities-for-the-artemis-missions); [SDT report PDF](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/artemis-iii-science-definition-report-12042020c.pdf)
- Site-relevant findings: science return is "intrinsically linked" to the landing site; proximity to geologic units enabling high-priority investigations (SPA and PSRs) is a key consideration; samples should be diverse and from geographically diverse locations representative of south-polar geology, including subsurface and volatile-bearing samples in sealed containers; pre-positioned assets and mobility recommended; long-lived power/communications for post-crew monitoring. — [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/here-are-nasas-science-priorities-for-the-artemis-missions); [SpaceQ](https://spaceq.ca/nasa-releases-artemis-iii-science-definition-team-report/)
- Sample mass: NASA requirement at least 35 kg, goal 100 kg; SDT scenarios of 25 kg and 83 kg; Apollo average 64 kg; LEAG recommended >= 150 kg. [Sources differ on which figure is "the" target] — [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/?p=108995); [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/artemis-astronauts-will-be-doing-some-serious-science-o-1845834921); [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/here-are-nasas-science-priorities-for-the-artemis-missions)

### Inferences
- Per-region science scoring can be grounded as follows: **SPA-rim massifs** (Malapert Massif, Mons Mouton, Mons Mouton Plateau) score on objective 1/3 (ancient crust, basin chronology — the new 4.25 Ga anchor makes an independent SPA-rim sample more, not less, valuable as a cross-check); **Shackleton rim / Connecting Ridge / de Gerlache Rim 2** score on a datable Late-Imbrian crater with abundant mapped boulders plus immediate PSR access; **Haworth, Nobile Rim, Peak near Cabeus B** score on proximity to the largest/coldest pre-Nectarian PSRs and (Cabeus) the only ground-truth volatile site.
- Because the largest PSR floors have ~3.5 Ga retention ages, "oldest volatile record" should not simply be equated with "oldest host crater".
- Absence of pits near the pole means natural-shelter credit (lava tubes) should be zero for all sites poleward of 80 S; topographic shielding by crater walls is the available substitute (see hazards).

## 5. Hazards with site-specific data (slopes, boulders, regolith, plume, dust, seismicity, radiation, micrometeoroids, cold)

### Takeaway
Hard published limits: land within 6 deg of the pole, slope <10 deg at 100 m scale (8 deg contours used; crewed landed tilt <= 8 deg), EVA terrain <20 deg at 2 km scale, direct-to-Earth visibility, lit surface for the stay, landing within 100 m of target. Site-specific red flags: Shackleton's ~29-30 deg walls and modelled regolith-landslide susceptibility; young thrust-fault scarps inside de Gerlache Rim 2 and ~30 km from Nobile Rim 1 with modelled Mw ~5.3 shaking out to 40-50 km; abundant boulders on the Shackleton rim/ridge; PSR regolith properties uncertain (70% porosity inferred vs normal bearing capacity from boulder tracks); and demonstrated landing difficulty on Mons Mouton (IM-2). Radiation is ~1.4 mSv/day unshielded, with crater walls cutting SEP dose by >= 40%.

### Inferences
- Hazard flags by region, derivable from the above: **de Gerlache Rim 2** — contains the largest young scarp near the pole (seismic source inside the region) plus an unstable PSR slope to its north; **Nobile Rim 1** — ~30 km from the Shoemaker scarp, unstable interior walls; **Shackleton rim / Connecting Ridge** (not in the 2024 nine but relevant for Chang'e-7 and base studies) — steepest walls, landslide-susceptible edges, dense boulders, but the best crater-wall radiation shielding and PSR access; **Mons Mouton / Mons Mouton Plateau** — broad plateau but the only site with a failed precision landing on record (IM-2), caused by navigation rather than proven terrain properties; **Malapert Massif** — >5 km relief with a ~3.5 km cliff nearby, so local slope maps are decisive.
- A distance-to-nearest-scarp term (thresholds ~40 km "strong-moderate", ~50 km "light" for an Mw ~5.3 event) and a slope-instability mask (>30 deg at low cohesion) are directly supportable by Watters et al.; recurrence rate of such events is not quantified, so the weight should be modest.
- PSR trafficability should be scored as "uncertain", not "soft": the only mechanics-based evidence (boulder tracks) indicates normal-to-higher bearing capacity.

### Gaps
- No site-by-site published tables of boulder density, crater density or slope statistics for the nine 2024 regions were retrieved (products exist in PGDA 78 and LPI atlases but were not readable here).

- Surface-potential values for PSRs/terminator (kV range) not confirmed from a primary Farrell et al. source; a -3.8 kV nightside figure appeared only in a secondary summary.
- Annual south-pole surface dose with solar-cycle variation, and site-specific sky-view shielding factors for the candidate regions, not found.
- Shallow-moonquake recurrence probability for the south pole not quantified in sources found.

## 6. Published multi-criteria site rankings combining science value and hazards

### Takeaway
Several GIS-based rankings exist, each with different criteria and none official. Volatile-focused studies (Lemelin et al. 2014; Flahaut et al. 2020) favour PSR interiors/margins such as Shoemaker, Faustini, Haworth, de Gerlache, Cabeus and Amundsen; the only study that ranks NASA's 13 Artemis regions with an explicit MCDM method (Pena-Asensio et al. 2024, TOPSIS) puts **Nobile Rim 2** first, followed in top-100 share by Malapert Massif and Amundsen Rim.

### Inferences
- Published rankings split into "volatile-first" (PSR-centred; ignore crew landing constraints) and "operations-first" (Artemis regions; science reduced to geologic diversity/mafic abundance). None includes seismic hazard (Watters 2024), contamination persistence (Moores 2025, Saxena 2026) or SPA-rim sampling value explicitly — an app combining these would be novel rather than reproducing an existing scheme.
- The Pena-Asensio result is sensitive to its 0-5 deg slope pre-filter and to the number of surviving points per region (473 in Nobile Rim 2 vs 20 at Haworth); it should be treated as one input, not a consensus ranking.
- Of the 2024 nine regions, only Haworth, Malapert Massif, Nobile Rim 1/2 and de Gerlache Rim 2 were present in the 13-region set that study analysed; Peak near Cabeus B, Mons Mouton, Mons Mouton Plateau (partly ex-Leibnitz Beta Plateau) and Slater Plain have no published multi-criteria score found.
