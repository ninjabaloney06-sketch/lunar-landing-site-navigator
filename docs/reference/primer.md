Lines marked **(derived)** are arithmetic on cited numbers. Audit fixes from [primer-audit.md](../archive/primer-audit.md) applied 10 Nov 2026; derived numbers re-computed then and all match.

## Why lighting is extreme at the poles

- The Moon's equator is nearly aligned with the ecliptic. Its rotation axis is tilted **6.68°** to its orbit, and the orbit is inclined **5.145°** to the ecliptic ([NASA NSSDC Moon Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html)). The measured effect is what counts here:
- The subsolar point (solar zenith point) never leaves a narrow band around the equator. In NASA SVS's hourly 2026 "Dial-A-Moon" data the subsolar latitude ranges from **−1.557° to +1.586°** ([mooninfo_2026.json](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/vis/a000000/a005500/a005588/mooninfo_2026.json), behind [SVS 5588](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5588); main page [SVS 5587](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5587)).
- At the south pole, the elevation of the Sun's *centre* above a flat, airless horizon is **minus** the subsolar latitude **(derived)**: the Sun circles the horizon within about ±1.6° and never climbs higher. The Sun is a disc, not a point ("our light source is not a point but rather a finite disc", [PGDA 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69)), so its upper limb rises a little higher. Because of the Moon's "low obliquity and heavily cratered terrain", local relief decides lit versus shadowed: "Peaks of near-eternal sunlight tower above nearby permanently shadowed regions (PSRs)" ([Barker et al. 2023, PSJ 4, 183, open access CC BY 4.0](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1)). Poleward of 80° S the total PSR area is **20,800 km²** for PSRs of at least 1 pixel (20 m); 20,600 km² for PSRs of at least 5 pixels (same paper).
- Consequence for the challenge (our planning statement): lighting at a south-pole site is a *terrain* question, so it must be answered with a horizon mask over a DEM (below), cross-checked against published illumination products ([PGDA 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69), which cites Mazarico et al. 2011, Icarus 211, 1066–1081).

## Why Earth hovers near the horizon

- The sub-Earth latitude is "the lunar latitude at which the Earth is directly overhead" ([NASA SVS 5199](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5199)). Libration in latitude, an apparent north–south nodding of the Moon, is "mostly an effect of the tilt of the Moon's orbit relative to its equator" (**6.68°**), in "a cycle lasting about 27.12 days" (same SVS page). Over time "up to 59%" of the surface becomes observable from Earth ([NASA Stargaze: Librations of the Moon](https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/stargaze/Smoon4.htm)).
- In the 2026 SVS data the sub-Earth latitude ranges from **−6.795° to +6.856°** (about ±7°); sub-Earth longitude ranges **−7.936° to +7.594°** (same JSON as above).
- At the south pole, Earth's elevation above a flat horizon is **minus** the sub-Earth latitude **(derived)**: Earth stays within about ±7° of the horizon and is *below* it during the part of the ~27-day cycle when the sub-Earth point lies in the Moon's northern hemisphere. A polar direct-to-Earth link therefore needs a horizon-clear direction toward Earth during the specific window, not just "Earth is up".
- Two corrections for real sites **(derived)**: (a) the SVS sub-Earth point is geocentric (Moon centre to Earth centre); seen from the lunar surface, Earth's direction shifts by up to asin(1737.4 / 356,648 km) ≈ **0.28°** (radius from the fact sheet, minimum distance from the 2026 JSON). (b) Earth's disc has an angular radius of asin(6378.1 km / distance) ≈ **0.90–1.02°**.

## What direct-to-Earth (DTE) comms needs

- (Our reasoning from the section above, not a sourced statement.) DTE needs an unobstructed line of sight from the site to Earth for the whole communications window. Near the pole Earth is always low, so terrain in Earth's azimuth can break the link even when Earth is geometrically above the horizon. PGDA supports the general point: "landing sites or traverse planning may require direct radio contact" ([PGDA 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69)).
- NASA treats this as a landing criterion: "communication capabilities with Earth" is one of the factors named in selecting the Artemis south-pole landing regions ([NASA news release, 28 Oct 2024](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-provides-update-on-artemis-iii-moon-landing-regions)).
- Published reference products exist for exactly this question: PGDA's lunar polar illumination/visibility maps include an "Average Earth visibility" product for "mission planning"/"radio contact", computed from LOLA topography, in which "the Earth is considered visible if any part of its disc is not obscured". The page itself warns the product "may be very slightly optimistic for radio link studies" ([PGDA product 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69)).
- Relays (orbiters, surface nodes) are the usual workaround when DTE fails; for our tool the question stays "when is DTE possible".

## What a horizon mask is

- A **horizon mask** for a site is the terrain-caused elevation limit as a function of azimuth: for each compass direction around the site (360 directions is our design choice), the lowest elevation angle at which local terrain blocks the sky. It is built by casting rays from the site over a DEM (e.g. the LOLA south-pole LDEM) and taking the maximum terrain elevation angle along each ray, lunar curvature included. Barker et al. 2023 compute "the angular elevation of the local horizon ... in all directions for every pixel", using nested maps from 20 m/px out to 7 km and up to "480 m pixel−1 at a distance of 310 km" ([Barker et al. 2023](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1)). Our plan (TASKS.md phase 4) uses 100–150 km; S checks at the event that this is enough.
- Visibility rule: an object is terrain-visible at time t iff elevation(t) > mask(azimuth(t)), for the Sun often refined to "any part of the disc", for Earth likewise (PGDA's Earth-visibility definition above). Published illumination and Earth-visibility maps are made in a similar way from LOLA topography ([Barker et al. 2023](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1) describe the horizon computation; [PGDA 69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69) cites Mazarico et al. 2011 for its maps).

## Coordinate frames: which one we use

Three lunar body-fixed frames exist in SPICE ([frame kernel `moon_de440_250416.tf`](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/generic_kernels/fk/satellites/moon_de440_250416.tf), [NAIF Frames Required Reading](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/toolkit_docs/C/req/frames.html)):

- **MOON_PA** (principal axes, alias of MOON_PA_DE440, 31008): axes along the principal axes of inertia; "the Z axis of this system does not coincide with the Moon's mean spin axis". The binary PCK `moon_pa_de440_200625.bpc` "provides the orientation of the lunar Principal Axes (PA) reference frame" ([PCK comments](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/generic_kernels/pck/moon_pa_de440_200625.cmt)). Used for dynamics and gravity.
- **MOON_ME** (mean Earth / polar axis, 31001): +Z "aligned with the north mean lunar rotation axis, while the prime meridian contains the mean Earth direction" (frame kernel). A *constant* rotation away from PA: (67.8526″, 78.6944″, 0.2785″) about axes 3, 2, 1 for DE440. Current MOON_ME is an alias of `MOON_ME_DE440_ME421`, which matches DE421's ME frame to about **53.4 cm** for 2000–2040 (same kernel).
- **IAU_MOON**: the mean-Earth frame from the IAU/IAG report's truncated rotation formulas (`pck00011.tpc`). It "does not agree closely" with DE-based MOON_ME: up to ≈ 0.0051° (**≈ 155 m**), 0.00239° (≈ 72 m) on average for 2000–2040 (frame kernel). **It is the SPICE default** body-fixed frame for the Moon wherever no frame is named.

**IAU vs. mean Earth** are not competing systems: both the IAU/IAG and LRO use the mean Earth/polar axis *system*; ME use "is consistent with the recommendations of the IAU/IAG Working Group" ([LRO/LGCWG white paper v5](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/luncoordwhitepaper-10-08.pdf)). They differ in *realization* (IAU_MOON: analytic formula; MOON_ME: JPL ephemeris).

**Size of the error if mixed.** ME and PA differ by "approximately 0.02886 degrees", ≈ 875 m on a great circle (frame kernel). That is the maximum. **(derived)** Near the south pole the 78.69″ tilt term dominates, ≈ **0.66 km**; the 67.85″ spin term adds only 0.57 km × cos(latitude), ≈ 0.05 km at 85° S. So mixing them shifts a polar site by roughly **0.7 km**, many 20 m DEM pixels: the DEM gets sampled at the wrong place and "north" points slightly differently.

**Our convention.**

1. **Sites, DEM, horizon mask, map: MOON_ME**, planetocentric latitude/longitude on the 1737.4 km sphere. "All the LOLA polar products use a stereographic projection centered at the pole in the MOON_ME frame of the JPL ephemeris DE421" ([Barker et al. 2023](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/PSJ/acf3e1); same on [PGDA 90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90)). Site sources do not name a frame; all are LRO-based and LRO delivers ME planetocentric coordinates, so ME is **inferred, not stated**. VIPER (Beyer et al. 2025) states planetocentric "Moon (2015) - Sphere / Ocentric". Confirm with a mentor at the event.
2. **Ephemeris:** load `de440s.bsp`, `moon_pa_de440_200625.bpc`, `moon_de440_250416.tf`; request Sun and Earth vectors directly in `'MOON_ME'` (the kernel's own example: `SPKEZR('Earth', ET, 'MOON_ME', 'NONE', 'Moon', …)`). Then build the local east-north-up frame from the site's MOON_ME latitude/longitude. DE440-ME vs. DE421-ME (53 cm) is negligible next to 20 m pixels.
3. **Never use IAU_MOON**: always name `'MOON_ME'` explicitly (legacy `SUBSOL`/`SUBPT`/`ET2LST` default to IAU_MOON; use `subslr`/`subpnt` with the frame argument). Convert PA→ME only by rotating body-fixed vectors with `pxform('MOON_PA', 'MOON_ME', et)`, never by editing latitude/longitude numbers.
4. **Validation:** before comparing with JPL Horizons, find out which lunar frame Horizons uses for a surface observer, or a 0.7 km site offset will look like an ephemeris error (open, for S's Horizons task).
